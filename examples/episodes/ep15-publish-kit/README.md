## Description
Why do highways use thin steel cables to stop runaway cars? A car that crosses the median is headed straight into oncoming traffic. So three tensioned steel ropes run between weak posts. When a car hits them, the posts fold over and the cables stretch back like a slingshot, about eight feet. That long stretch slows the car gently instead of stopping it dead, and the tension steers it back along the median, away from the other lanes. It's not a wall. It's a giant steel net.

Simplified animation for explanation, not a crash test.

## Claims check
- High-tension cable median barriers use three or four tensioned steel wire ropes on light posts that are meant to bend or break away
  when hit; the film shows three.
- Deflection: dynamic deflection of cable barriers in crash tests is typically several feet up to about 8-12 ft depending on post
  spacing and tension; the model peaks at 7.8 ft ("about eight feet").
- Gentler than a rigid wall: the car is slowed over the long stretch (model peak about 2.7 g at 60 mph, 25 degrees) instead of in a few
  inches; the cables then redirect the car along the barrier (model: it ends on its own side, x -8.6 m).
- Purpose: cross-median crashes into oncoming traffic; cable barriers are installed in medians to prevent them.
