## Description
Drive over this road and it plays a song. It's cut with grooves like a giant vinyl record: each time a tire hits a groove it makes a thump, and hundreds of thumps a second blur into one note. Close grooves make a high note and wider gaps a lower one, so the spacing writes a melody into the road, and if you drive slower the song slows down and drops in pitch. In Lancaster, California, a musical road plays the William Tell Overture. But the crew measured the gap between grooves instead of groove to groove, so every note came out flat, the high notes most of all. That's why it's famously out of tune.

## Claims check
- How it works: grooves cut across the lane; a tyre crossing them at speed v hits v / spacing grooves a second, and that rate is the
  pitch (f = v / s). At 55 mph the tune's repeated note (B, 247 Hz) needs grooves about 3.9 in apart.
- Lancaster, California: built in 2008 for a Honda Civic commercial, plays the finale of Rossini's William Tell Overture (the Lone
  Ranger theme); the first version on Avenue K was paved over after neighbours complained about the noise and it was rebuilt on
  Avenue G; it is meant to be driven at 55 mph (Atlas Obscura, NBC Los Angeles, KQED, ABC10).
- Why it is out of tune: the spacing in the instructions was cut as the gap between grooves rather than measured groove to groove,
  so each spacing grew by one groove width (Tom Scott's video drawing on David Simmons-Duffin's analysis; Laughing Squid; Morton's on
  the Move). Adding the same width to every spacing lowers the close (high) notes the most, so the tune is not just lower but wrong.
- The tune: the opening phrase of the William Tell Overture finale (Rossini, 1829, public domain), synthesized from the model's tyre
  thumps; the road in the film is a simplified stand-in, not a survey of the real grooves. The groove width (3/4 in) is an
  assumption; with it the built road plays 3 to 5 semitones flat.
