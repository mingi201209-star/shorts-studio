## Description
Why does the end of a guardrail peel into a steel ribbon when a car hits it?

Because a plain rail end doesn't give way: it spears through the front of the car, right into the cabin. So the ends of highway guardrails get a steel head. Hit it, and the head slides down the rail. Inside, it squeezes the rail flat and bends it out of the way, and the force it takes to crush that steel is what slows the car down. The posts near the end snap off, so nothing snags the wheels. Sixty to zero in about two car lengths, and the cabin is untouched.

So next time you pass a yellow guardrail head, you know what it's waiting for.

This is a simplified physics model made for explanation, not a real crash test: a 1,400 kg car at 60 mph, a constant crushing force, breakaway posts. Real terminals, cars and crashes vary.

## Claims check
- Blunt guardrail ends could spear through vehicles into the passenger compartment: the historical reason energy-absorbing end terminals exist (model: the rail goes 2.3 m into the car, past the front of the cabin at 0.95 m).
- Extruding terminal: the head is pushed down the rail, flattens it and bends it out of a side slot: general description of extruder-type terminals.
- The crushing force slows the car: model, 56.7 kN constant after a 0.25 m build-up; 60 mph to zero over 9.0 m = 2.2 car lengths of 3.99 m ("about two car lengths"), average 4.1 g.
- Breakaway posts near the end: general description; model: five posts snap.
- "The cabin is untouched": model, the nose dents 0.12 m against the head plate, the crumple zone is 0.95 m.
