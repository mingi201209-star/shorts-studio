## Description
Same car, same wall. One at 30 mph, one at 40. Only ten miles an hour apart.

Crash energy grows with the square of your speed, so those ten extra miles an hour carry 78% more energy. All of it has to go somewhere: the steel crush rails in the front fold like an accordion and turn it into bent metal and heat. At 30 they crush about 20 inches. At 40, nearly 28, almost all the way to the cabin.

Hitting a wall at 30 mph is like falling off a three-story building. At 40 mph it's a five-story fall. And it starts before the crash: with the same driver and the same brakes, where the 30 mph car comes to a stop, the 40 mph car is still doing 35.

So in town, those ten miles an hour aren't a little faster. They're almost twice the crash.

This is a simplified physics model made for explanation, not a real crash test. It uses a 1,400 kg car with a linear crush stiffness, a 1.5 s reaction time and 0.8 g braking; real cars, crashes and stopping distances vary.

## Claims check
- Energy ½mv²: (40/30)² = 1.78, +78 %: arithmetic.
- Crush depth grows in proportion to speed with a linear crush stiffness (common first-order model in crash reconstruction): model; 40 mph crush set to 70 cm (27.6 in), 30 mph then 52.5 cm (20.7 in).
- Equivalent fall height h = v²/2g: 30 mph -> 9.2 m, 40 mph -> 16.3 m; with about 3 m per storey, 3 and 5 stories: arithmetic (stated as "like").
- Stopping with 1.5 s reaction and 0.8 g: 30 mph stops in 31.6 m; at that point the 40 mph car is at 35.0 mph: model formulas (same as EP.10).
- "Almost twice the crash": 1.78 x the energy.
