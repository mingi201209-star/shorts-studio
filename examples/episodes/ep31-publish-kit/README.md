## Description
Why is this road spraying water in a snowstorm? In snowy parts of Japan, sprinklers are built right into the street. Nozzles down the middle of the road spray out water pumped from deep underground, where it stays around fifty degrees. That's warmer than snow, so the snow melts as soon as it lands. The idea started in the city of Nagaoka in the 1960s. It works there because the snow falls near freezing, so the water doesn't turn to ice. The catch is it uses lots of water and can make the ground sink, so newer roads keep the warm water in pipes under the pavement.

Simplified animation for explanation; not a specific street.

## Claims check
- Snow-melting pipes (shosetsu pipes) run down the centre of the road, pump up groundwater and sprinkle it onto the surface; they
  are common in Niigata's snow country (The Autopian: https://theautopian.com/how-japan-clears-snow-from-the-roads-without-salt-and-plows;
  Core77: https://core77.com/posts/135763/Japans-Clever-Anti-Snow-Roadways).
- Japan's groundwater is about 8-18 C all year, the range the system needs; the model uses 12 C (54 F, "around fifty degrees").
- The system began in Nagaoka: one paper dates it to 1961, a Cambridge paper to about 1963 after a very heavy snowfall
  (https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/product/50096754845E5A385EFD24482E954C07/core-reader); the film says "the 1960s".
- It needs groundwater and mild winters so the water does not freeze, which the Niigata area satisfies; sprinkling wastes water and
  has caused groundwater shortages and land subsidence; newer systems use heat-radiating pipes under the pavement with groundwater
  as the heat source (same sources; the iron in the groundwater also stains the roads orange-brown, as drawn).
- The model (0.30 L/min per square metre of 12 C water against 2.5 cm of snow an hour: it melts 2.7 cm an hour; the film stays
  liquid with the air near 0 C and would freeze at -10 C; about 180 m3 an hour for a kilometre of street; pipes giving 250 W/m2
  keep up with the same snowfall) is a simplified explanation model, not a design guide. The well and the sinking ground are drawn
  larger than life.
