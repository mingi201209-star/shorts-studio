## Description
How does a net stop a twenty-ton boulder? Mountain roads hang steel nets on the slopes above them, woven from steel rings like chain mail. When a rock hits, the net doesn't try to stop it dead: it stretches like a trampoline, more than twenty feet. The ropes that hold it pull through brake rings, steel loops that shrink and bend and turn the speed into heat. A slow stop keeps the forces low, so nothing snaps. The biggest nets stop twenty tons at sixty miles an hour; afterward, crews clear the rock and replace the brakes.

## Claims check
- Flexible rockfall barriers: ring nets of steel wire rings (e.g. 300 mm rings of 3 mm wire), posts, support and retaining ropes,
  and brake elements in the ropes that absorb most of the impact energy by deformation and are replaced after an event. Energy
  classes range from about 50 kJ to more than 8,000 kJ (e.g. Geobrugg RXE-8000 tested at 8,000 kJ). Sources: Swiss FOEN product data
  sheets (Geobrugg ROCCO-2000, RXE-8000, RXI-500); Cerro et al. (ACG) on brake elements; ETAG 027 / EAD 340059-00-0106 (MEL / SEL).
- 20 t at 60 mph is 7.2 MJ (7,200 kJ), inside the top class.
- Braking distance in the model: 8.4 m (27 ft); test approvals allow larger braking distances (the RXI-500 certificate lists up to
  15 m at 5,000 kJ), so "more than twenty feet" is within real behaviour.
- "Turning the speed into heat": brakes dissipate energy by plastic deformation and friction.
- The model (20 t, 60 mph, a net force rising with the square of the deflection, then brakes slipping at a near-constant force) is a
  simplified explanation model; no product's data are claimed.
