## Description
Why does this bridge peel trucks open like a can? It's a rail bridge with only 11 feet 8 inches of room, so a steel crash beam out front takes the hits instead of the bridge. A box truck's roof is aluminum thinner than a dime, riveted over thin ribs, so the steel wins: the rivets pop and the roof peels back while the lower cab rolls right under. This truck is 13 feet: 16 inches too tall. Now a sensor spots tall trucks and turns the light red, and in 2019 the bridge was raised 8 inches. Trucks still hit it, just less often.

Inspired by the Norfolk Southern–Gregson Street Overpass in Durham, North Carolina (the "11foot8" bridge). Simplified animation for explanation; not a reconstruction of any real crash.

## Claims check
- Clearance 11 ft 8 in; raised 8 in to 12 ft 4 in in October 2019; a steel crash beam in front of the trestle protects the bridge (it
  does not protect the trucks); crashes continue, less often. Sources: Wikipedia, "Norfolk Southern–Gregson Street Overpass";
  Construction Equipment Guide, "Raising a Bridge That's Set Too Low for Traffic"; The News & Observer via Yahoo News, "A legendary
  Durham bridge still mauls trucks, though not as often as it used to".
- The over-height system: when it detects a tall vehicle it lights an "OVERHEIGHT, MUST TURN" sign and turns the signal before the
  bridge red (NCDOT, 2016). Source: FHWA Office of Operations case study on over-height vehicle detection. The film shows beacons and a
  turn arrow instead of the words (no text in the picture).
- Box truck roof skin: 0.040 in (1.0 mm) aluminium is a common spec (federal truck body spec minimum; Utility Trailer, Great Dane
  practice), over roof bows on about 24 in centres. A US dime is 1.35 mm thick.
- The truck in the film is a generic 13 ft box truck (most US states allow up to 13 ft 6 in); its cab (2.95 m) is below the beam.
  The crash model (15 mph, an 8,000 kg truck, a header crush pulse, a steady peel force and the brakes after 0.7 s) is a simplified
  explanation model; it is not presented as measured data and no number from it is quoted except the heights.
