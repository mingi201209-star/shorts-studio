## Description
Same puddle, same speed. Only one car can still steer, and the only difference is tread depth.

Those grooves are drains that squeeze water out from under the tire. New tires have about 8 millimeters of them; worn to the legal limit, just 1.6 (2/32 inch in most US states). Then the water can't escape fast enough: it piles up into a wedge and lifts the tire off the road. Now you're water skiing, and steering and brakes barely work.

NASA even has a rule of thumb for when it starts: nine times the square root of your tire pressure (psi) gives the speed in miles per hour. At 32 psi that's about 51 mph (82 km/h), and worn tires float even sooner. Check yours with a penny: put Lincoln's head into a groove, and if you can see the top of his head, it's time. If you start to float, ease off the gas and keep the wheel straight until the tires bite again.

This is a simplified physics model made for explanation, not a real test. The rule of thumb comes from NASA tire research (Horne) and is an estimate for deep water; real onset depends on water depth, tire, load and speed. The coin in the film is a plain coin, drawn for illustration. Tread sections are shown 10x larger.

## Claims check
- New tread about 8 mm: typical new passenger tyres have roughly 8 to 9 mm (10/32 to 11/32 inch).
- Legal limit 1.6 mm (EU) / 2/32 inch (most US states).
- Water wedge lifts the tyre (dynamic hydroplaning): standard description.
- Rule of thumb v ~ 9 sqrt(p) mph: Horne (NASA), for full hydroplaning of a tyre; stated as a rule of thumb, with the onset lowered for worn tread in the model.
- Penny test: standard US advice (2/32 inch at the top of Lincoln's head).
- What to do: ease off the accelerator, no hard braking, keep the wheel straight: standard driving advice.
