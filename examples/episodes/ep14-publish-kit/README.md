## Description
Why does a car window explode into tiny cubes? It's tempered glass: heated red hot, then blasted with cold air. The surface hardens first, then the inside cools, shrinks and squeezes the surface tight. That squeeze makes it about four times stronger than normal glass, but the energy stays locked inside. Break through the surface and it all lets go at once: cracks race across the pane at nearly a mile per second, leaving blunt little cubes instead of knife-like shards. Windshields are different: glued to plastic, they crack but hold together. That's why emergency hammers are made for side windows.

## Claims check
- Tempering: heated to about 620-650 C, quenched with air jets; the surface sets first, the core cools later and leaves the surface in
  compression, the core in tension: standard description of thermal tempering (model: surface -100 MPa, core +50 MPa).
- "About four times stronger": fully tempered glass is commonly given as roughly four times the strength of annealed glass.
- Crack speed about 1,500 m/s = 0.93 mile per second ("nearly a mile per second"): typical crack branching speed in soda-lime glass.
- Cubes vs shards: tempered glass dices into small blunt pieces; annealed glass breaks into long shards (model: median aspect 1.35 vs 2.7).
- Windshields are laminated (two sheets on a plastic interlayer) and stay in one piece when cracked; emergency hammers are meant for
  tempered side windows. Some newer cars also use laminated side glass, so the description says "made for side windows", not "every".
