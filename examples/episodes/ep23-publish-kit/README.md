## Description
Why do manhole covers sometimes blast into the air? Under many city streets, power cables run through underground vaults. In winter, salty meltwater drips onto old cables, and the salt eats at the insulation until electricity arcs. The burning insulation gives off flammable gas that fills the vault. One spark, and the gas explodes, and it can launch a cast-iron cover weighing up to 300 pounds. So New York's utility is swapping in covers with vents: the gas escapes through the holes instead of building up. Other covers are latched: they pop up a few inches to vent, then drop back into place.

## Claims check
- Cause: road salt and snowmelt seep into underground electrical structures and damage cable insulation; the cables arc and the
  smouldering insulation gives off gases that can build up and ignite (Con Edison; news coverage of New York and Boston events).
  Events peak in the winter months.
- Weight: covers weigh up to about 300 lb (reports give 85-300 lb); "up to three hundred pounds".
- Vented covers: Con Edison has been replacing solid covers with vented ones so heat and gas can escape (more than 121,000 vented
  covers installed); "New York's utility is swapping in covers with vents".
- Latched covers: Eversource's "energy-release" covers in Boston lift about 3-4 inches, two latches on the rim catch the cover and it
  reseats (WBUR, 2022); Con Edison also fits latches to some manholes. "They pop up a few inches, vent, and drop back."
- The model (an 11.6 m^3 vault, gas from smouldering insulation, a lean mixture burning through the vault, orifice venting around
  the lifting cover) is a simplified explanation model: under a solid cover the gas reaches its flammable limit after about 8
  minutes, under a vented cover it levels off at 2 % (39 % of the limit); the blast peaks at 70 kPa (10 psi) and throws the cover
  21 ft up; a latched cover is caught at 3.5 in and back on its seat in 0.47 s. No figure from it is claimed as data.
