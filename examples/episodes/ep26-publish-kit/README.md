## Description
Why is this guardrail covered in spinning yellow rollers? It's a rolling barrier made in South Korea for dangerous curves. Hit a plain steel rail and it scrapes and drags at the car. These rollers are barrels of tough foam on steel posts. When a car slides into them they spin instead of scraping. The foam squashes to soak up the hit, and the spinning turns part of the crash into rotation. So the car keeps rolling forward and is steered back toward its lane. One version passed a 62 mph crash test for American roads, and Korea already has more than 60 miles of them.

## Claims check
- Made in South Korea (ETI), best suited to high-risk sites on curves and at highway entrances and exits; EVA rollers, highly shock
  absorbent; over 100 km in use on Korea's road network ("more than sixty miles") (World Highways, 2019:
  https://www.worldhighways.com/wh11/products/new-roller-barrier-korean-firm-eti).
- The rollers spin on posts and turn the impact into rotation, steering the car back instead of stopping it (Interesting
  Engineering: https://interestingengineering.com/this-new-korean-rolling-barrier-system-could-save-millions-of-lives).
- "One version passed a sixty-two mile an hour crash test for American roads": FHWA eligibility letter B-291, Road Safety Barrier
  Roller System ET1-GR02-TL4, tested to MASH Test Level 4 (KARCO); MASH TL-3/TL-4 car and pickup tests run at 100 km/h (62 mph) and
  25 degrees (https://highways.dot.gov/sites/fhwa.dot.gov/files/2022-08/b291.pdf). Roller barrier transitions to W-beam also hold
  MASH TL-3 letters (B-252A, B-336).
- The model (a 1,500 kg car at 100 km/h and 25 degrees into the rollers and, for comparison, into a plain steel rail) is a
  simplified explanation model: on the rollers the car keeps 88 % of its speed and leaves at 9 degrees back toward its lane; 25 kJ
  goes into the rollers' spin (a quarter of the 103 kJ the crash puts sideways), 61 kJ into the foam; the steel rail's scraping
  takes 196 kJ and the car keeps 73 %. Not the test report of any product.
